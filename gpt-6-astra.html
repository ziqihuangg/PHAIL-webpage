GPT-6-Astra as an embodied policy

gpt-6-astra is OpenAI's general flagship model, with no robotics-specific training and no motor outputs of its own. In the three weeks since it shipped, six groups have wired it to a robot through their own harness and published what happened. This page holds all of it in one place: what each group ran, the clips, and — at the bottom — the four questions they do not agree on.

Naming note: this “Astra” is OpenAI's. Unrelated to Google DeepMind's Project Astra, which this site files separately, under environment-perceiving agents rather than physical actuation.

    Who ran what

    RoboDojo's report defines RoboProbe, a three-level taxonomy for “what occupies the action path.” We reuse its vocabulary because it is the clearest way to say what is actually comparable. L1: a trained policy acts, no language model anywhere. L2 / Hybrid: the model directs a separately-trained policy, which still emits every action. L3 / Direct: the model's own output drives the robot through a fixed, non-learned harness — no trained checkpoint in the loop. Where a source does not state its level or reasoning effort, the cell says “not stated” rather than guessing.

    They are less independent than they look. RoboDojo's L3 harness is an Inspect Robots agent, and Inspect Robots is Robocurve's own open-source framework — which Robocurve then used, at version 0.58.0, for its evaluation. Su et al. built their own harness but re-tabulate RoboDojo's published board for all their public reference columns rather than re-running them. Six sources, closer to three or four independent measurements.

    RoboDojo Team: the operator's own run

    The only source here that is a benchmark running its own harness on its own board. Three frontier models were put through an identical L3 interface — GPT-6-Astra and GPT-5.5 at 50 episodes per task, DeepSeek-Flash at 10 — so any gap between them is the model, not the wire format.

    Sim leaderboard, official protocol

    The three L3 rows are the same harness with a different planner. GPT-5.5 lands at rank 35 and DeepSeek-Flash at 30: the result is specific to this model, not to “a frontier LLM.” Score is mean process reward × 100; Average SR is the equal-weight mean of the five axes below, not the micro rate.

    Where the lead comes from

    Success rate per axis on the official 42-task protocol. RoboDojo scores five things separately instead of collapsing them, and the profile is the opposite shape to a trained policy's. Astra leads on memory and open-ended instruction following — the axis where nearly every VLA is near zero — and is last-tier on precision at 4.00%.

    An average of 22.48% is a mixture of two populations, not a policy that solves everything two times in ten. Geometric alignment, numbered or algebraic instructions and language-conditioned sorting go to Astra; continuous contact and multi-step physical scripts go to the VLAs. Best-VLA column names: Xiaomi-Robotics-1, GalaxeaVLA (G0.5), DM0.5, OpenWAM-α.

    Real robots: the campaign that was stopped

    Safety stop. No complete official RoboDojo-Real campaign exists. Per the report, testing was halted after Astra repeatedly issued physically unreasonable or unsafe actions, including incidents that damaged equipment. What survives is 33 diagnostic trials across 12 tasks and three robots, retained before and around that stop — not a submission, and not comparable to the official 18-task board.

    One full success in 33 trials. The zero-score clips are not blank: they generally show approach, grasp or transport attempts that never meet the terminal predicate.

    What survives an unannounced change mid-episode

    One episode per layout across eight general_pickup layouts, same L3 harness, 100 planner calls unless noted. The harness silently perturbs the world and does not tell the model. It recovers from a negated action space and mirrored cameras by comparing what it asked for against what happened. It does not recover from missing global vision or 10 cm of control jitter, because searching and re-grasping consume the episode budget.

    On the piano runs the model writes a play.py rather than acting through the harness — no training, no reference solution, learning a controller from practice data explicitly forbidden and audited. The delivered program then generalises over tempo but not over key geometry:

    Same program, same 0.05 s control loop, same official note-onset metric, re-run without re-optimisation. An unseen C major scale (white keys only) beats the score it was written for; a D major scale drops to 0.556. A program written for one score generalises over tempo and over the key geometry it was built against, and not beyond it.

    Su et al.: alone vs correcting a VLA

    The most directly useful study on this page for anyone deciding how to build with the model. One team, one week, two architectures on one interface: Astra emitting bimanual end-effector targets itself, or Astra reviewing a 50-step π0.5 candidate and choosing to accept it or overwrite 1–5 steps. Episodes are paired instance by instance on task, scene, layout and seed.

    This report was anonymised when first posted; it now carries named authors (Su, Zheng, Yan, Yi, Zhang, Wang) and a citation. A community index attributes it to a specific lab — the report itself states no affiliation, so we do not either.

    Mean RoboDojo Score over ten tasks, stratified towards ones π0.5 already fails. The hybrid takes the top of the board at Score 62.60 and 48% success; Astra alone lands mid-pack at 37.81 and 26%; π0.5 alone is at 24.43 and 15.67%. Grey rows are recomputed from RoboDojo's published per-task board over the same subset, not re-run.

    The efficiency result is the counter-intuitive one: the hybrid is both better and cheaper. Astra rewrites 14.4% of executed control steps and the run costs 44.8% fewer tokens, because it no longer has to construct low-level motion segment by segment. Physical duration excludes model-response latency, which the report names as the unresolved obstacle to real-time deployment.

    And then the ordering reverses

    Same authors, same week, a different benchmark — RoboLab on a single-arm Franka — and direct control wins, 98% to 92%. The reason is in the small print: the RoboDojo runs use π0.5 weights fine-tuned on those tasks, while the RoboLab baselines are DROID weights transferred zero-shot, where π0.5 alone manages 36%. A student worth correcting helps; a student that is wrong two times in three mostly creates work.

    Robocurve: 40 trials, human-graded

    The smallest quantitative source here and, for that reason, the cleanest. Two tasks on bimanual YAM arms, the same agent policy and rubric used for an earlier Fable comparison, and every trial graded 0–4 by a human on how far it got — so a failure still records where it stalled.

    Domain note: published at openai.robocurve.org, a subdomain of robocurve.org. Robocurve is a third-party evaluator and this is not an OpenAI-owned domain; we found no stated OpenAI affiliation on the page.

    The token column is the striking one, and it is confirmed on the primary page rather than second-hand: 2.1k output tokens per bowl run against Fable 5.1's 12.9k — roughly 6× fewer — while completing 19 of 20 against 8 of 20. On the puzzle task it stalls at the same final step Fable does, just faster and cheaper.

    openai.robocurve.org/gpt-6-astra · Menon et al., 2026-09-04

    GPT-Policy: learning from context

    The only source that systematically varies what you put in the prompt rather than what the model is: five families of context — human video, robot video with and without action labels, a goal image, the model's own interaction history, and live human gestures — across ten real-robot tasks, three trials per condition. No gradient updates anywhere.

    Three trials per cell, so read the direction rather than the rate. The direction is consistent: every no-context row is 0/3, and adding a demonstration of the same scene moves it to 2/3 or 3/3 — usually with fewer decision-steps, not more. Goal images, self-history and live interaction are 3/3 on all six of their tasks.

    The project's own third finding is the honest one: “moves take time.” A run that succeeds still takes 8–25 minutes of wall clock and tens of model decisions, which is why its authors position VLM agents for reasoning and replanning rather than fast low-level control.

    Innate: one episode as the context

    Two artefacts from the same team, which is why they sit in one section rather than two: a 20-second clip posted by Axel (@ax_pey) on 2026-09-11, and the pull request that implements it.

    The skill, imitate_demonstration, records one episode — synchronised joint positions, forward-kinematics end-effector poses and camera frames — and makes it the model's whole context. No trained policy, no fitted trajectory, one bounded and validated decision per observation, with fresh telemetry re-read before any servo motion. Reasoning effort low.

    Two details make it more than a demo. First, the ablation is built into the interface: leave the task field empty and the model must infer the objective from the recording alone. On a four-part task — knock the plant off its platform, drop the rod, pick up the unit, put it back — the inferring variant reportedly recovered all six phases from the episode and named objects it had not been told about. Second, the posted clip claims transfer across a changed environment, camera angle and layout with no text prompt at all, with the model choosing per step whether to command end-effector or joint space.

    No success rate is claimed, and we are not inferring one. These are demonstrations: one run each, no n, no protocol, no failure count. They appear here because the mechanism is specific and checkable in the diff, and because they are the clearest statement of the position that the RoboDojo demonstration study contradicts — see the last section.

    Awesome-Astra: 35 community cases

    Awesome-Astra-Embodied-AI is a discovery index, not a benchmark — it points at other people's demos rather than running anything. It earns a place here for shape rather than scores: it shows which uses people reached for first.

    The distribution is the interesting part. Only one of the 35 cases uses Astra the way most deployed systems would — as a planner calling a trained policy. Twenty-two put it directly in the action path, and twelve use it for something that is not control at all: rebuilding scenes from video, or writing and tuning the RL environments that train other policies. Entries are dated 2026-09-05 to 2026-09-16; every one is a single clip from a single author.

    Where the six disagree

    The reason to gather six reports on one page is not to average them. It is to find the places where two careful groups ran the same model and got opposite answers — and then to work out which difference in protocol produced the gap.
