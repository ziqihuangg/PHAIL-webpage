Physical AI Ledger

What counts as physical AI

AI whose output is realised in the physical world, split by what that output is: an action carried out by a robot, a car or a drone (execution layer), or a design that gets manufactured - a part, an assembly, a circuit board (planning layer). Models that only perceive or predict sit on the boundary. Hover a board for what it measures; a dot marks a live leaderboard, L a board whose numbers are in our ledger.

Introducing PhAIL

Artificial Analysis answers “which model is actually good?” for language, image, video and speech with one independent, quantitative index. Physical AI has nothing like it, and not by accident: you cannot call a robot the way you call an API, every board runs different tasks on different hardware, and most published numbers are graded by the people who built the model.

PhAIL - the Physical AI Ledger, pronounced like “fail”, because honest evaluation starts with what breaks - builds a small, honest piece of that picture in three layers: the map above of what is measured and where; a ledger of published results that records who produced every number; and a draft cross-board ranking that says how sure it is.

It is a research notebook run out of NTU / MMLab, not a company. We have no robots and grade nothing ourselves: we read published tables, copy them without renormalising, and attach the citation and protocol to every value. If something here is wrong or missing, we would like to hear about it.

How the ranking works

  1. Boards, not papers. Only tables where the benchmark operator ran every model on one harness feed the default index - six robotics boards today.
  2. Compare inside a board only. A raw 30 on one board and 60 on another are never added. Each board contributes “A beat B”, discounted when the gap is inside the board's own sampling noise.
  3. Pool the comparisons. A Bradley-Terry model, the same maths as Chatbot Arena, turns every pairwise result into one strength per model, even though no two models share the same boards.
  4. Weight by difficulty. A saturated board barely counts; a board where the best model is at 34% counts most. Boards from one operator share a weight.
  5. Show the doubt. Every model carries its board coverage and the range its index moves through when any one board is left out.

What is still missing

In the evaluations

  • No shared harness: every board is its own task set, robot and rollout count, so models are compared on whatever boards they happened to enter.
  • No speed or cost: none of the six benchmark-run boards reports inference latency, control frequency or hardware.
  • One number per board: only RoboDojo scores capabilities (memory, precision, ...) separately.
  • Post-trained, not general: most boards test a policy fine-tuned on that board's own demonstrations; zero-shot transfer is rarely measured.
  • Small real-robot boards: 5 to 16 models each, and they do not always agree with each other.
  • Safety sits outside the ranking: refusal and incident boards (RoboHarm) cover a handful of models.

In the models (ledger, 27 Sep 2026)

  • Open-ended instructions: 43 of 45 trained policies score below 5 on RoboDojo's open-vocabulary axis. GPT-6-Astra, driving the arm through a harness, scores 34.
  • Robustness: on RoboTwin, Fast-WAM goes from 77.8% on clean scenes to 1.9% under randomisation.
  • Embodiment: 10 of 11 policies on RoboDojo Real score lower on Piper X than on Piper; three score zero on at least one arm.
  • Memory: 5 of 45 trained policies clear 30 on RoboDojo's memory axis, 37 stay below 10.
  • Absolute level: the best simulated policy completes 28% of RoboDojo episodes; the best real-robot entry 24%.

How every number is labelled

  • Benchmark-run. The operator ran the model on its own harness and published it - RoboChallenge, RoboDojo, RoboTwin, RoboArena. Strongest class; outside submitters are named.
  • Self-reported. The model's authors, on a harness they controlled. Not wrong by default - several are reproducible from a released checkpoint - but a different kind of claim.
  • Third-party. Someone else ran or re-tabulated it, usually as a baseline in a paper whose point is to beat it.
  • No citable number. An evaluation is claimed or expected but nothing can be cited. Kept visible: a blank is information.

Anything we compute rather than copy - the index, percentiles, a mean over suites - is drawn in violet and marked as derived. Affiliations come from each paper's title block, first institution first; logos are each lab's own and remain their trademarks. What we are working on

  • Keeping boards current. Automatic reads of live leaderboards, with a dated change log.
  • New domains in the ledger. One board each for driving, drones and CAD design.
  • Validating the index. Held-out boards, and blinded re-runs of the pairs the boards disagree on.
  • Capability scores across boards. Tagging tasks on every board with the capabilities they test.
  • Speed and cost. Asking operators to publish latency and hardware beside success.
  • A design index. Frontier models on CAD and PCB boards, ranked the same way.
  • A composite suite. A non-redundant sample of tasks from the open boards.
  • A longer write-up. What physical AI can and cannot do yet, read off the ledger.
