Robotics Index

Ranks count every fitted model, so a filtered view keeps the original numbers. Each model's best entry on each board is used.

Index method

Why
No single board ranks the field, each board alone is noisy, and the boards share enough signal that the pooled index predicts a left-out board as well as the best single board while judging more pairs - see Board agreement.
Data
Published results from 6 leaderboards whose operators run every model themselves, on one protocol - 121 results for 85 models, copied unchanged from the Ledger. We measure nothing ourselves; the models' own paper tables are off by default.
Method
Inside each board, every pair of models is compared: who scored higher, and by more than the board's own noise? Boards are weighted by how much room they leave at the top. All comparisons are then pooled into one strength per model, which puts models on one scale even when they never met on the same board.
Meaning
A model's index is the share of head-to-head match-ups it is expected to win against every other ranked model: 50 is the middle of this field, 90 beats nine in ten. It is relative to the models ranked here - not a success rate, and not an absolute skill level.

Each step opens for details. Their numbers are for the default settings and are recomputed from the Ledger on every load.

  1. Seven boards, each run by its operatorRoboDojo Sim and Real, RoboChallenge T30 and T30-v2, RoboTwin 2.0, RoboArena, PAW-GEN-10. Paper tables join only when switched on, at half weight. RoboChallenge's closed competitions stay in the Ledger only; the reasons are under Boards and weights.
    Details

    Intuition. Only tables where one operator ran every model under one protocol - the same tasks, robots and number of trials - are comparable within the table. A number a model's own authors published on their own set-up is a different kind of claim, so it stays out unless the Evidence switch is on.

    • Each model's best entry on a board is used, as the board itself shows it.
    • RoboChallenge T30-v2 entries that ran fewer than 15 of its 30 tasks stay in the Ledger only (the board counts unrun tasks as zero).
    • With Evidence = "+ paper tables", each benchmark's paper tables are pooled into one extra board, at half weight and an assumed 500 trials.
  2. Never add scores across boardsA board only says who beat whom. 34 on RoboDojo and 64 on RoboChallenge are different scales.
    Details

    Intuition. Boards differ in tasks, robots, number of trials and in what a point is worth: a success rate, a progress score, an Elo rating. The top of one board can sit at the bottom of another. So a raw score is only read against the other scores on the same board - which model is above which, and by how much relative to the noise (next step).

    The Method switch's "Raw mean" does exactly what this step forbids - a weighted average of raw scores - so the difference can be seen.

  3. Small gaps count as tiesP(A beats B) = Φ(gap / noise), with noise from the board's trial count. A 1-point gap over 180 real trials is only about 60/40.
    Details

    Intuition. A board runs each model a limited number of times, so every score carries sampling noise. A gap smaller than that noise is close to a coin flip, so it counts as a near-tie rather than a clean win; a gap several times the noise counts as a full win.

    noise of one score\(\displaystyle \mathrm{SE} = 100\,\sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{n}}\)
    \(p\) = score / 100, kept between 0.02 and 0.98; \(n\) = trials per model
    noise of a gap\(\displaystyle \sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_A^{2} + \mathrm{SE}_B^{2}}\)
    win share\(\displaystyle \Pr(A \text{ beats } B) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\text{score}_A - \text{score}_B}{\sigma}\right)\)
    B gets \(1 - \Pr(A \text{ beats } B)\)
    normal curve\(\displaystyle \Phi(0) = 0.50, \quad \Phi(1) \approx 0.84, \quad \Phi(2) \approx 0.98\)

    Reading an Elo rating (RoboArena)

    Intuition. RoboArena has no success rate. Evaluators run two anonymous policies on a task of their choice and say which did better; every vote is one match. Ratings are fitted so that the gap between two ratings predicts which policy is preferred, as in chess. Only gaps mean anything: the level (around 1,500) is arbitrary, so there is no ceiling and no best score to compute a difficulty from - step 4 gives Elo boards a neutral 0.5.

    preferred\(\displaystyle \Pr(A \text{ preferred over } B) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-(R_A - R_B)/400}}\)
    rules of thumb\(\displaystyle R_A - R_B = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.50, \qquad 100 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.64, \qquad 400 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.91 \;\; (10 \text{ to } 1)\)
    link to step 6\(\displaystyle s = 10^{R/400}\)
    the same Bradley-Terry curve, with strength written on a base-10 scale

    RoboArena also publishes a standard deviation per policy - the uncertainty of its own fit - and the index uses it as SE in the formulas above.

  4. Harder boards count moreWeight = 1 − best score / 100: LIBERO (best 97%) ≈ 0, RoboDojo Sim (best 34%) ≈ 0.66. One operator's boards share one weight.
    Details

    Intuition. A board whose best model already scores 97% can no longer tell good models apart; one whose best scores 34% still has room. Two boards from the same operator measure much the same thing, so they share one vote. An operator-run table counts twice as much as a paper's table.

    difficulty\(\displaystyle d = 1 - \frac{\text{best score}}{100}\)
    kept between 0.02 and 1; Elo boards have no ceiling, so \(d = 0.5\)
    family share\(\displaystyle f = \frac{1}{\text{number of boards from the same operator in view}}\)
    evidence\(\displaystyle e = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{operator-run board} \\ 0.5 & \text{pooled paper tables} \end{cases}\)
    weight\(\displaystyle w = d \cdot f \cdot e, \qquad \text{share of the index} = \frac{w}{\sum_{\text{boards}} w}\)
  5. Big boards don't outvote small onesEach model receives a board's weight once, however many rivals share that board.
    Details

    Intuition. A board with many models produces far more pairs than a small one. If every pair counted the same, the biggest board would decide the ranking on its own.

    weight per pair\(\displaystyle \omega = \frac{w}{k - 1}\)
    on a board with \(k\) ranked models
    weight per model\(\displaystyle (k - 1) \cdot \frac{w}{k - 1} = w\)
    each model sits in \(k - 1\) pairs, so it receives the board's weight exactly once, whatever \(k\) is
  6. Pool with Bradley-TerryOne strength per model from every pairwise result - the maths behind Chatbot Arena.
    Details

    Intuition. Think of Elo ratings in chess, or Chatbot Arena for language models. Each model gets one hidden number, its strength. When two models meet, the stronger one usually wins, and the bigger the gap the surer the win. We do not set the strengths by hand: we choose the ones that best explain all the comparisons from steps 3-5 at once. Comparisons chain - A beat B on one board, B beat C on another - so the fit can place A above C even if A and C never met. Models that sit on two or more boards are those links.

    model\(\displaystyle \Pr(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{s_i}{s_i + s_j}, \qquad s_i > 0\)
    log scale\(\displaystyle \beta_i = \ln s_i: \qquad \Pr(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_i - \beta_j)}}\)
    the logistic curve Elo ratings use (step 3)
    data\(\displaystyle \omega_{ij} = \frac{w}{k - 1} \;\text{(steps 4-5)}, \qquad W_{ij} = \text{win share (step 3)}\)
    fit\(\displaystyle \max_{s} \; \sum_{\text{pairs}} \omega_{ij} \left[ W_{ij} \ln \frac{s_i}{s_i + s_j} + (1 - W_{ij}) \ln \frac{s_j}{s_i + s_j} \right]\)
    update\(\displaystyle s_i \;\leftarrow\; \frac{\sum_j \omega_{ij} W_{ij}}{\sum_j \omega_{ij} / (s_i + s_j)}\)
    repeated until nothing moves (Hunter's MM algorithm, 2004); plus a weak tie, weight 0.1, against a fixed reference of strength 1, so a model that lost - or won - every comparison still gets a finite strength
  7. Index = expected win rate0-100: the share of head-to-heads a model should win against every other ranked model.
    Details

    Intuition. Strengths have no natural unit. To make them readable, imagine each model playing every other ranked model once: its index is the share of those games it is expected to win.

    index\(\displaystyle \text{Index}_i = \frac{100}{N - 1} \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{s_i}{s_i + s_j}\)
    \(N\) = number of ranked models
    • Relative to this field: adding many weak models raises everyone's index. That is why RoboChallenge T30-v2 entries that ran few tasks are left out (step 1).
    • Not a success rate: an index of 94 does not mean 94% of tasks done.
  8. Show the doubtWhisker: lowest and highest index when any one board is dropped. Boards column: how many boards a model rests on.
    Details

    Intuition. With a handful of boards, one board can move a model a lot. So the whole fit is repeated once per board with that board removed, and the spread is shown instead of hidden.

    • Drop-one range (whiskers, table column): the lowest and highest index across the full fit and every refit that still contains the model. Rank range is the same for rank.
    • Held-out accuracy (Boards and weights): fit without board X, take the pairs on X whose gap exceeds their noise, and count how often the refit orders them the same way. 50% is a coin flip.
    • Boards column: a model on one board rests entirely on that board. Board agreement (below) compares the order two boards give their shared models.

For comparison only, on the Method switch: Percentile mean ignores how strong each board's field is; Raw mean is the plain weighted sum of published scores.

Boards and weights

  • Held-out accuracy: fit without the board, then check its clearly separated pairs. 50% is a coin flip; red is below 60%.

Board agreement

  • Kendall's τ over the models two boards share (four or more).
  • +1 same order, −1 reversed. Red cells lean towards reversed; the details show which τ could be chance.
Details: what τ means and how it is computed

Intuition. Take the models two boards have both ranked and look at every pair of them. If both boards put a pair in the same order, the boards agree on that pair; if they put it in opposite orders, they disagree. τ is the share of agreeing pairs minus the share of disagreeing pairs. It uses only the order, never the raw scores - the same rule as the index - so a success rate can be compared with an Elo rating.

pairs\(\displaystyle \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}\)
\(n\) = models both boards ranked; C = pairs both boards put in the same order, D = pairs they put in opposite orders, \(T_A, T_B\) = pairs tied on only one of the two boards (pairs tied on both are skipped)
Kendall's τ-b\(\displaystyle \tau_b = \frac{C - D}{\sqrt{(C + D + T_A)(C + D + T_B)}}\)
with no ties\(\displaystyle \tau = \frac{C - D}{n(n - 1)/2}, \qquad \text{share of pairs in the same order} = \frac{1 + \tau}{2}\)
  • +1: identical order. 0: no relation - whether the boards agree on a pair is a coin flip. −1: exactly reversed.
  • τ = 0.5 means the boards order about 3 pairs in 4 the same way; τ = −0.5, about 3 in 4 the opposite way.
  • Few shared models make τ very uncertain: with 4 models there are only 6 pairs, and one flipped pair moves τ by 0.33. Even for two unrelated boards τ scatters by about \(\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{2(2n + 5)}{9n(n - 1)}}\) - the chance level below. A τ inside twice that could be luck.
  • Computed on each board's published values (each model's best entry), not on the index. Boards need 4 or more shared models to get a τ.

Pooled index against any single board

  • Leave one board out, fit the index on the others, and check the left-out board's clearly separated pairs: how many can it judge, and how many does it order correctly?
  • Compared with the single other board that shares the most models with it. If pooling helps, the index judges more pairs at equal or better accuracy.

Index against model size

  • No board publishes model latency or hardware; PAW-GEN-10 fixes control at 30 Hz and reports task time, not inference time.
  • Parameter count is the only proxy, and most models do not state it.
